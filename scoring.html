How The Draft
Order Was Scored

The rule, the reasoning behind it, and the raw results it was applied to. Everything here can be checked against the screenshots on pages 3 and 4.

Two games decided the order in which managers choose their draft slot. The scoring problem was that the two games produce numbers on wildly different scales, so the obvious approach — add up everyone's points — would have quietly handed the entire draft to one of them.

The rule

Only your finishing place in each game counts. Your raw score does not. points = (number of players + 1) − your place Nine managers played, so first place in a game is worth 9 points and last is worth 1. Add your Kahoot points to your Quiplash points. Highest combined total picks earliest. Ties break on the better Kahoot finish.

Worked example

Jack Meehan won the Kahoot, so 9 points. He came second in the Quiplash, so 8 points. Total 17, the highest of the night, so he picks first among those who played.

Scott Pender won the Quiplash for 9 points but came third in the Kahoot for 7. Total 16. One point behind Jack despite winning a game outright — because Jack was near the top of both.

Why not simply add the scores

Kahoot awards roughly a thousand points per question and pays a speed bonus on every one. The Quiplash round awards a hundred points per vote received. The actual spreads on the night:

GameWinner scoredLast scoredSpread
Kahoot15,035015,035
Quiplash2,3008001,500

Adding those together would make the Kahoot worth ten times the Quiplash round. The trivia would have decided the draft on its own and the second game would have been decoration — two dozen answers written and voted on, for nothing. Using places instead gives each game exactly the same influence: nine points to the winner of each, one to the last of each.

The other option, and why it was rejected

Normalising each game to a percentage of its winner's score sounds fairer, but it rewards the shape of a scoreline rather than performance. A runaway Kahoot winner would still swamp the field, and a game where everyone finished close together would count for almost nothing. Places are immune to that: they measure only who beat whom, which is the thing actually being contested.

The property that matters. Under this rule, winning either game is worth the same. There is no way to win the draft order by being enormously good at one game and poor at the other — you have to be good at both, which is what happened. Jack placed 1st and 2nd. Scott placed 3rd and 1st. Nobody else placed in the top three of both, and nobody else came close to their totals.

Why the middle five tied

Because a total is 20 − (Kahoot place + Quiplash place), any two managers whose places add to the same number score the same. Five managers all added to twelve:

They are mirror images of one another — each strong in one game and weak in the other by precisely the offsetting amount. This is not a bug in the scoring; it is the scoring working. All five genuinely performed the same overall. With nine players there are only seventeen possible totals, so the middle of the table is crowded by design.

Why ties break on the Kahoot

The two games measure at very different resolutions. Kahoot scores are effectively continuous — 15,035 against 12,888 against 12,415, no two alike, because the speed bonus separates everyone to the millisecond. Quiplash scores move in blocks of 100 and produce frequent exact ties: two managers finished on 1,800 and two more on 800.

When two measurements disagree, the finer one settles it. That is the whole justification. The Kahoot can distinguish between any two players; the Quiplash often cannot.

Audit: the same rule, applied to the Quiplash ties

Jack Meehan and JJ Woolley both finished the Quiplash on 1,800 votes. Kofi White and Tim DeVaney both finished on 800. Applying the tie-break rule to those pairs:

Where this is weakest

Stated plainly, because a result that hides its own soft spots is not worth defending:

  • Nine players, not twelve. Steven Erbrick and Joe Manganelli did not take part, so slots 11 and 12 were never contested. Steven Shuldman did not play and did not need to — MAGA MARALAGO awards the completed Waffle House challenge the first selection outright.
  • Places are coarse. Winning the Quiplash by 500 votes counts the same as winning it by 100. That is the deliberate cost of weighting both games equally.
  • The ninth Kahoot entry. It is nicknamed “Scott”, scored zero, answered nothing correctly and left two questions blank — while Scott Pender played as “Scott 2” and finished third. That zero was attributed to JJ Woolley. If it was his, the table stands. If he never joined the Kahoot, his ninth place is a placeholder rather than a measurement, and he has a legitimate case for a rerun of that half.
